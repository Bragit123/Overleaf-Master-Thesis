\section{Method}

\subsection{The Numerical Integration}
The method used for numerical integration differs between the \gls{qed} and \gls{qcd} results obtained in this thesis. Although the specific integrator will not affect results, this discrepancy deserves a brief mention.
% We should give a quick note on the numerical integration used to produce the plots presented in this chapter, as this has been done differently for our \gls{qed} results than for the \gls{qcd} results we will compare with.

The \gls{qcd} corrections were obtained using the currently unpublished \texttt{C++} library \texttt{smoking}~\cite{smoking}, which uses Monte Carlo integration, specifically the \texttt{Vegas} algorithm~\cite{Lepage:1977sw} implemented in the \texttt{C++} library \texttt{GSL}~\cite{Galassi:2019czg}.
For the \gls{qed} corrections, however, we have used the deterministic integration algorithm \texttt{Cuhre}~\cite{Berntsen:1991ciy} implemented in \texttt{Cuba}~\cite{Hahn:2004fe,Hahn:2014fua}.
As stated in the original \texttt{Cuba} paper, \texttt{Cuhre} is "quite powerful in moderate dimensions"~\cite{Hahn:2004fe}, and by explicitly testing both we found that \texttt{Cuhre} vastly outperformed \texttt{Vegas} for the relatively simple low-dimensional integrals relevant here, making it the integrator of choice in this thesis.
% This integrator was chosen because it is far more efficient than \texttt{Vegas} for the relatively simple low-dimensional integrals relevant in this thesis---we only consider integrals that are at most three-dimensional, integrating over the parton momentum fractions $x_1$ and $x_2$ and the invariant slepton mass $Q^2$.
% The high performance of \texttt{Cuhre} for low-dimensional integrals is also mentioned in the original \texttt{Cuba} paper~\cite{Hahn:2014fua}.


\subsection{Renormalization Schemes and Input Parameters}
% As discussed in \cref{sec:renormalization_schemes}, we have some freedom in choosing what renormalization scheme to use for the various parameters that appear in the numerical evaluation.
As discussed in \cref{sec:renormalization_schemes}, we have some freedom in choosing what renormalization scheme to use for the various parameters that appear in our calculation.
We will now review the specific choices made for the numerical implementation producing the results to be discussed shortly.

% In order to numerically evaluate the expressions we have found in \cref{ch:parton_level,ch:hadron_level} we need to set a number of input-parameters.
% All numerical calculations were done with a \gls{com} energy of $\sqrt{s}=\qty{13.6}{TeV}$.

We renormalize the \glspl{pdf} in the \gls{msbar} scheme, as we have already seen when eliminating the collinear divergence in \cref{sec:hadronic_cross_section}.
This scheme is chosen to match that of the \texttt{PDF4LHC21\_40} \gls{pdf} set~\cite{PDF4LHCWorkingGroup:2022cjn}, which we have used to obtain the \glspl{pdf} numerically in \texttt{C++}.

We want to compare the \gls{qed} corrections found in this thesis to state-of-the-art corrections in \gls{qcd}.
For the latter, the strong coupling $\alpha_s$ also enters the computations. Like the \glspl{pdf}, the strong coupling is also renormalized in the \gls{msbar} scheme, and its running is dealt with by \texttt{LHAPDF}~\cite{Buckley:2014ana}.

In using the Breit-Wigner propagator for the $Z$ boson, we have implicitly assumed its mass to be given in the complex-mass scheme.
This scheme is useful in that it regulates the cross section near resonance, and also has the advantage of giving slightly more accurate results in general as it comes from a resummation of \gls{1pi} diagrams, and consequently includes some information from the all-orders result.
In the complex-mass scheme, the $Z$ mass is

\end{equation}

where $m_Z = \qty{91.188}{GeV}$ is the $Z$ boson's real pole mass, and $\Gamma_Z = \qty{2.4955}{GeV}$ its total decay width.
Their numerical values are obtained from Ref.~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2026aaa}.

The electric charge and the Weinberg angle are closely related to the $W$ and $Z$ boson masses.
After electroweak symmetry breaking, these parameters are given by
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    m_W &= \frac{1}{2} vg,
    \\
    m_Z &= \frac{1}{2} v\sqrt{g^2+g'^2},
    \\
    e &= \frac{gg'}{\sqrt{g^2+g'^2}},
    \\
    s_W &= \frac{g'}{\sqrt{g^2+g'^2}} = 1 - \frac{m_W^2}{m_Z^2},
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $g$ and $g'$ are the weak and hypercharge couplings, and $v$ is the Higgs \gls{vev}.
Taking $m_Z$ and $m_W$ to be the pole masses, with $m_W = \qty{80.369}{GeV}$~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2026aaa}, we also fix the Weinberg angle as a direct consequence.

The remaining electroweak parameter is then the electric charge $e$, or equivalently the electromagnetic coupling $\alpha=e^2/(4\pi)$.
For this we will use the $G_\mu$ scheme, as described in \cref{sec:renormalization_schemes}, where
\begin{equation}
    \alpha \equiv \frac{\sqrt{2}G_\mu m_W^2}{\pi} \biggl(1-\frac{m_W^2}{m_Z^2}\biggr).
\end{equation}
The Fermi constant $G_\mu$---given in \cref{eq:Gmu_in_div} and restated here for the reader's convenience---is~\cite{ParticleDataGroup:2026aaa}
\begin{equation}
    G_\mu = \qty{1.166 378e-5}{GeV^{-2}}.
\end{equation}
As discussed in \cref{sec:renormalization_schemes}, the $G_\mu$ scheme has the advantage of absorbing the largest corrections to the electromagnetic coupling at the electroweak scale, and from the running of the Weinberg angle~\cite{Denner:2019vbn}.

Like all couplings, $\alpha$ runs with the energy scale. Nevertheless, the running of $\alpha$ is comparatively small when considering the running of the strong coupling---which appears in \gls{qcd} corrections that we will want to compare our results with---and the uncertainties from \glspl{pdf}. We will therefore assume $\alpha$ to be constant in our computation.

Lastly, we must include numerical values for the renormalization and factorization scales $\mu_R$ and $\mu_F$.
Although they are both unphysical parameters of which the cross section must remain independent, and can therefore be chosen freely, this statement is valid only when considering the full cross section at infinite order.
Working at finite order in perturbative \gls{qft}, the scales do not disappear from calculations, and the cross section is \emph{scale dependent}.

The conventional choice is to set both scales equal to some energy scale relevant to the calculation at hand.
In our case, we will set both scales to be equal to the average mass of the produced slepton pair, namely
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scale_central}
    \mu_0 \equiv \mu_R = \mu_F = \frac{m_{\tilde{\ell}_{iA}} + m_{\tilde{\ell}_{iB}}}{2},
\end{equation}
where $m_{\tilde{\ell}_{iA/B}}$ is the mass of slepton $\slepton_{iA/B}$.
The scale $\mu_0$ is dubbed the \emph{central scale}.
Keeping everything else fixed, and varying $\mu_R$ and $\mu_F$ around the central scale then allows us to evaluate how much the finite-order cross section depends on the chosen scale, which can in turn be used to estimate the result's uncertainty due to missing higher order terms.
We will now turn to a more general discussion of scale and \gls{pdf} uncertainty estimates.

In order to reliably compare predictions from various models with each other---and with experimental data from particle colliders---we need to estimate the uncertainty of the computed cross sections.

\subsubsection{The Scale Uncertainty}
The fact that the full all-order cross section must be scale independent makes $\mu$ a viable parameter to estimate the error of the cross section due to only working at finite order in perturbation theory.
% In general, one should vary the renormalization and factorization scale independently, which is what was done in Ref.~\cite{Braseth:2025ylj}.
% from which we will obtain the state-of-the-art \gls{qcd} corrections.
Following Ref.~\cite{Braseth:2025ylj}, we will estimate the scale uncertainty using a $7$-point scale variation---computing the cross section at each of the following renormalization and factorization scales:
% Here they use a $7$-point scale variation, recomputing the cross section for each of the following renormalization and factorization scales:
\begin{equation}
    \biggl( \frac{\mu_R}{\mu_0}, \frac{\mu_F}{\mu_0}\biggr) \in \biggl\{
        (1,1), \biggl(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\biggr), \biggl(\frac{1}{2},1\biggr), \biggl(1, \frac{1}{2}\biggr), (1,2), (2,1), (2,2)
    \biggr\} \equiv M,
\end{equation}
where $\mu_0$ is the central scale given by \cref{eq:scale_central}. From these evaluations, we can define the upper and lower bounds
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    \sigma_\mu^+ &= \max_{(\mu_R,\mu_F) \in M} \Bigl\{ \sigma(\mu_R, \mu_F) \Bigr\},
    \\
    \sigma_\mu^- &= \min_{(\mu_R,\mu_F) \in M} \Bigl\{ \sigma(\mu_R, \mu_F) \Bigr\},
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
which give the error band around $\sigma(\mu_0)$.

The $7$-point scale variation method is used to obtain the scale uncertainty of the \gls{qcd} results that we will compare our \gls{qed} results with.
For the \gls{qed} corrections themselves, it turns out that the cross section is independent of the renormalization scale, which somewhat simplifies the uncertainty estimation.

From \cref{eq:hadron_w_initial_state,eq:hadron_w_final_state} we see that the renormalization scale only appears in the soft $w$-function for the final-state correction.
Here it appears both explicitly in the $\ln(\mu_R^2/Q^2)$ term and in the Passarino--Veltman functions in $\Re\{g^{\text{finite}}(Q^2,m_1^2,m_2^2)\}$.
Extracting only the scale dependent terms in $g$, it can be shown that $\mu_R$ actually disappears from the full expression.\footnote{This has been verified in \texttt{Mathematica}. Note however that $\mu_R$ appears in $g$ as $\ln(\mu^2/m_i^2)$, so the full $\ln(\mu_R^2/Q^2)$ term doesn't cancel, only the $\mu^2$ dependence. We will still be left with terms of the form $\ln(m_i^2/Q^2)$ or $\ln(m_1^2/m_2^2)$.}
This result is independent of the sleptons' masses, and also holds for $m_1\neq m_2$.
In light of this, we will only vary the factorization scale $\mu_F$ when estimating the scale uncertainty at \gls{nlo} in \gls{qed}.
We therefore only compute the cross section at $\mu_F=\{\mu_0/2, \mu_0, 2\mu_0\}$---keeping $\mu_R$ fixed at the central scale $\mu_0$---and use the maximum and minimum values for the error bands.

\subsubsection{The PDF Uncertainty}
% The error due to uncertainties in the \glspl{pdf} is slightly more convoluted.
The \gls{pdf} uncertainty is slightly more convoluted.
As we have already mentioned, the \glspl{pdf} are obtained from the Hessian \gls{pdf} set \texttt{PDF4LHC21\_40}~\cite{PDF4LHCWorkingGroup:2022cjn}.
% Using \texttt{LHAPDF}~\cite{Buckley:2014ana}, we have obtained the \glspl{pdf} from the Hessian \gls{pdf} set \texttt{PDF4LHC21\_40}~\cite{PDF4LHCWorkingGroup:2022cjn}.
This \gls{pdf} set contains $43$ \gls{pdf} members, which we index by $k=0,\ldots,42$.
The zeroth member gives the central \gls{pdf}, used to compute the cross section result, while the remaining $42$ are used to estimate the \gls{pdf} error.
% Although the strong coupling $\alpha_s$ does not appear for the \gls{qed} corrections we have considered, it does appear at higher orders in \gls{qcd}, which we will want to compare with
% We therefore also need to estimate the cross section uncertainty due to $\alpha_s$.
% Following \cite{Butterworth:2015oua}, we can calculate the \gls{pdf} error estimate by

Following \cite{PDF4LHCWorkingGroup:2022cjn}, we calculate the \gls{pdf} uncertainty as
\begin{equation}
    \delta^{\mathrm{PDF}}\sigma = \sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{42} \Bigl(\sigma^{(k)} - \sigma^{(0)} \Bigr)^2},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma^{(k)}$ denotes the cross section found using the $k$'th \gls{pdf} member. $\delta^{\mathrm{PDF}}\sigma$ is then understood as a \qty{68}{\percent} confidence level~\cite{PDF4LHCWorkingGroup:2022cjn}.

Although the strong coupling $\alpha_s$ does not appear in \gls{qed} corrections, it does appear in the state-of-the-art \gls{qcd} corrections we want to compare results with.
The $\alpha_s$ uncertainty $\delta^{\alpha_s}\sigma$ is found for the \gls{qcd} corrections by using the \texttt{PDF4LHC21\_40\_pdfas} \gls{pdf} set, rather than \texttt{PDF4LHC21\_40}.
The \texttt{PDF4LHC21\_40\_pdfas} set contains two more members, used to estimate the uncertainty in $\alpha_s$.
Following the procedure described for estimating the \gls{pdf} uncertainty, but using the \texttt{PDF4LHC21\_40\_pdfas} set instead then gives the combined $\mathrm{PDF}+\alpha_s$ uncertainty
\begin{equation}
    \delta^{\mathrm{PDF}+\alpha_s}\sigma = \sqrt{(\delta^{\mathrm{PDF}}\sigma)^2 + (\delta^{\alpha_s}\sigma)^2}.
\end{equation}

Consider the production of a pair of left-handed selectrons with mass $m_{\tilde{e}_L}$. Since the produced selectrons have the same mass, the central scale in \cref{eq:scale_central} is simply $\mu_0 = m_{\tilde{e}_L}$.
We can then vary the scale around this value, and see how the total cross section changes with it. This is shown in \cref{fig:xsec_scale_ratio}.

As expected, there is no dependence on the renormalization scale $\mu_R$, as shown in the top-most plot of \cref{fig:xsec_scale_ratio}.
At \gls{lo} there are no divergences, so we don't need to vary the spacetime dimensions in the first place, and consequently the renormalization scale is never introduced, and does not appear in predictions.
At \gls{nlo} the renormalization scale cancels between the explicit $\ln(\mu_R^2/Q^2)$ term and the Passarino--Veltman functions, as discussed in \cref{sec:scale_and_pdf}.
We have only included one plot over renormalization scale in \cref{fig:xsec_scale_ratio} for illustrative purposes, as the corresponding plots for other selectron masses are identical.

The cross section does, however, depend on the factorization scale $\mu_F$, as evident from the remaining four plots in \cref{fig:xsec_scale_ratio}.
It is unsurprising that the factorization scale also affects the \gls{lo} cross sections. It enters in the \glspl{pdf} which are relevant when relating the partonic and hadronic cross sections at both \gls{lo} and \gls{nlo}.

The dependence on the factorization scale is almost identical at \gls{lo} and \gls{nlo}, although the \gls{nlo} result is shifting ever so slightly towards a more stable curve.
This is because the \glspl{pdf} enter in the same way for \gls{lo} and \gls{nlo}.
We have considered the running of the electromagnetic coupling to be negligible, keeping $\alpha$ constant, so the only place left for the factorization scale to make a noticeable difference is in the $w$-functions in \cref{eq:hadron_w_initial_state,eq:hadron_w_final_state}, where it only appears for initial-state corrections which contribute considerably less to the \gls{nlo} cross section than the final-state corrections.\footnote{This will be shown shortly.}

In addition, as we will soon see, the \gls{nlo} \gls{qed} corrections is at a size comparable to the \gls{pdf} uncertainty at \gls{lo}.
With such a tiny difference in the cross section compared to the leading cause of factorization scale dependence, it is not remarkable that the \gls{nlo} cross section depends on scale similarly to the \gls{lo} case.

For higher order corrections in \gls{qcd}, the scale dependence changes more violently at increased orders (see e.g.\ Ref.~\cite{Braseth:2025ylj}).
There the \gls{nlo} correction is sufficiently larger, and one must take into account the running of the strong coupling $\alpha_s$, which also affects the factorization scale dependence.
% For higher order corrections in \gls{qcd}, the contribution is significantly larger. Taking also the running of the strong coupling $\alpha_s$ into account, it is
% For higher order corrections in \gls{qcd} one must also take into account how the strong coupling $\alpha_s$ runs with scale. The variations in scale dependence at higher orders is then also more prominent~\cite{Braseth:2025ylj}.

It is also worth noting that the perturbative expansion in $\alpha$ also involves weak corrections. A real possibility is therefore that one needs to include the full set of electroweak corrections at \gls{nlo} to see a sizable decrease in scale dependence.
If the \glspl{pdf} become more accurate in the future, but the scale dependence does not improve significantly at \gls{nlo} in \gls{qed}, this could therefore hint at the weak corrections being larger than expected.
For the time being, with comparably large \gls{pdf} uncertainties, this is, however, only speculation.

We will consider the production of a pair of equal-mass selectrons in the process $pp\to\tilde{e}^*\tilde{e}+X$.
The \gls{com} energy is set to $\sqrt{s}=\qty{13.6}{TeV}$, so as to match Run~\Romannum{3} at the \gls{lhc}.
The total cross section for this process as a function of the selectron masses is shown in \cref{fig:xsec_over_mass}.
% The total cross section for the production of a pair of equal-mass selectrons as a function of their masses is given in \cref{fig:xsec_over_mass}.
The result is identical for smuons, which differ from selectrons only by their mass.
It is, however, worth noting that the same does not apply for the third-generation stau sparticles.
Staus have non-zero off-diagonal terms in the mass mixing matrix as discussed in \cref{sec:slepton_masses}, and thus behave differently from the selectrons and smuons.

The cross section in \cref{fig:xsec_over_mass} follows an exponentially decaying curve.
The energy required to produce a pair of massive particles such as selectrons increases when their masses are higher, so higher masses at a fixed \gls{com} energy means that the production is less likely to happen.
Consequently, the cross section decreases rapidly.
This is the main motivation for potentially building an even larger particle accelerator than the \gls{lhc}, namely the \gls{fcc}~\cite{FCC:2025lpp}---reaching higher energies and thus increasing the chances of observing high-mass particles (such as sleptons).
% This is the main motivation for the discussion of potentially building an even larger particle accelerator than the \gls{lhc}, namely the \gls{fcc}~\cite{FCC:2025lpp}---reaching higher energies and thus increasing the chances of observing supersymmetric particles with high masses.

This is because the right-handed selectrons do not couple to the $SU(2)_L$ gauge group, giving the left-handed selectrons slightly enhanced interactions in comparison.\footnote{Both left- and right-handed selectrons couple to the $Z$ boson, though, since it is a linear combination of the $B$ field from $U(1)_Y$ and the $W^3$ field from $SU(2)_L$. The coupling for left-handed selectrons is still stronger due to the $Z$ boson's underlying gauge structure.}

It is also clear from \cref{fig:xsec_Kfactor} that most of the \gls{nlo}~\gls{qed} contribution comes from the final-state corrections.
This can help understand why the \gls{qed} correction is larger than one might naively expect based on the corresponding \gls{nlo} \gls{qcd} correction.
% This is perhaps more evident in \cref{fig:xsec_ratio_qed_qcd}.
As we mentioned at the end of \cref{sec:initial_state}, the initial-state \gls{qed} correction at \gls{nlo} is nearly identical to the corresponding \gls{nlo} correction in \gls{qcd}~\cite{Tore:2022}. The only difference is a change in coupling $\alpha Q_q^2 \leftrightarrow \alpha_s C_F$, hence a naive expectation for the \gls{qed} correction would be (assuming\footnote{This is, of course, a very rough estimate. The strong coupling runs rather quickly with scale, making it hard to give a good general approximation. At the electroweak energy scale, with $\alpha_s(m_Z^2)\sim 0.118$, the value is closer to around $\alpha/\alpha_s \sim 0.06$.} $\alpha/\alpha_s \sim 0.1$ and averaging over valence quark charges $Q_q$)
% \begin{equation}\label{eq:naive_is_correction}
%     \sigma_{\mathrm{QED}}^{\mathrm{NLO}-\mathrm{LO}} \sim \frac{\alpha Q_q^2}{\alpha_s C_F} \sigma_{\mathrm{QCD}}^{\mathrm{NLO}-\mathrm{LO}} \sim 0.025\sigma_{\mathrm{QCD}}^{\mathrm{NLO}-\mathrm{LO}},
% \end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:naive_is_correction}
    \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{QED}}^{\mathrm{NLO}-\mathrm{LO}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{QCD}}^{\mathrm{NLO}-\mathrm{LO}}} \sim \frac{\alpha Q_q^2}{\alpha_s C_F} \sim 0.025,
\end{equation}
which is not much.

The final-state corrections do not have a \gls{qcd} counterpart. The sleptons are color-neutral, and hence do not obtain \gls{qcd} corrections at \gls{nlo}. This is the main qualitative distinction between \gls{qed} and \gls{qcd} corrections, allowing more diagrams to contribute to the \gls{qed} correction.
When we added virtual and emitted photons to the final-state, the additional interaction vertices did not involve quarks, but were proportional to $e$ rather than $eQ_q$.
These contributions are therefore slightly stronger than the initial-state corrections. A slightly better naive expectation for the final-state corrections is therefore to only swap the couplings $\alpha \leftrightarrow \alpha_s$, giving a \gls{qed} contribution of approximately one tenth of the \gls{qcd} correction.
Although more accurate for the final-state correction, this approximation is more naive in the sense that the final-state \gls{qed} corrections are qualitatively different from those of \gls{qcd}, where the corresponding diagrams do not exist.
% Although more accurate for the final-state correction, this approximation is more naive in the sense that the final-state \gls{qed} corrections are qualitatively different from \gls{nlo}~\gls{qcd}, since the corresponding diagrams do not exist within \gls{qcd}.
We would therefore not expect this approximation to be particularly accurate.

In \cref{fig:xsec_ratio_qed_qcd} we have plotted the ratio of \gls{qed} to \gls{qcd} corrections at \gls{nlo} (only the correction, not including \gls{lo}), to see more clearly how they differ.
We see that the naive expectation for initial-state corrections in \cref{eq:naive_is_correction} fits very well with the actual correction.
The small deviation is expected, since we have given a very rough estimate for $\alpha_s$ and not taken its running into account.\footnote{The \gls{qcd} correction also includes supersymmetric corrections and the contribution from quark--gluon hard scattering, which also affect the result.}
% The small deviation is expected, since the \gls{qcd} correction includes supersymmetric corrections, the contribution from quark--gluon hard scattering, and we have given a very rough estimate for $\alpha_s$ and not taken its running into account.
The more general naive expectation of shifting only the coupling $\alpha_s\to\alpha$ is less accurate, which is not surprising given the discussion above.
Nevertheless, it is closer to the full \gls{nlo}~\gls{qed} correction than the initial-state approximation.
The true \gls{qed} correction is actually notably larger than the naive expectation suggests, at approximately \qty{16}{\percent} that of \gls{nlo}~\gls{qcd}.
