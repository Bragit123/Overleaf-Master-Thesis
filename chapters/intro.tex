\chapter*{Introduction}
\markboth{Introduction}{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}

The Standard Model of particle physics is a \acrfull{qft} describing the fundamental particles that make up the universe, and their interactions.
It has achieved remarkable success, describing three of the four forces of nature---the electromagnetic force and the strong and weak nuclear forces---and having predicted the existence of new particles such as the Higgs boson---included in electroweak theory by Weinberg~\cite{Weinberg:1967tq} based on the mechanism by Higgs~\cite{Higgs:1964ia,Higgs:1964pj,Higgs:1966ev} (and independently by Englert and Brout~\cite{Englert:1964et}), and discovered nearly $50$ years later at CERN~\cite{ATLAS:2012yve,CMS:2012qbp}.

Despite its success, there are still questions the Standard Model fails to answer.
It does not explain the nature of dark matter, which makes up the majority of matter content in our universe; it does not incorporate gravity, which is needed to understand regimes such as the very early universe or black holes; and it does not explain the large discrepancy between the expected and observed Higgs mass, known as the Higgs hierarchy problem.
In the pursuit of explanations to these problems, we are lead to explore new theories \acrfull{bsm}.

One of the most well-explored frameworks for constructing \gls{bsm} models is to allow fermionic states to transform into bosonic states, and vice versa, and require the Lagrangian to remain invariant under such transformations.
This is known as supersymmetry.
The smallest possible supersymmetric model---in the sense of containing the least amount of particles---that still contains all of the Standard Model is named the \acrfull{mssm}~\cite{Martin:1997ns}.

In the \gls{mssm}, each Standard Model particle receives at least one supersymmetric partner---or sparticle---with opposite spin-statistics, but otherwise identical properties.
In particular, the sparticles should have the same mass as their Standard Model partners, and should therefore already have been observed.
To explain the absence of sparticles in experiments, supersymmetry must therefore be broken at low energies, allowing the sparticles to acquire higher masses than their Standard Model partners.
Without knowing the breaking mechanism, this is accounted for by introducing explicitly supersymmetry breaking terms in the Lagrangian, which leads to an abundance of parameters, making comparison between model and experiment incredibly difficult.

Searching for supersymmetry at particle colliders such as the \gls{lhc} typically consists of calculating the cross section for different production processes at various points in parameter space, using statistical analysis and simulations of collisions to compare predictions with experimental data, to find which parameter values can be confidently excluded~\cite{ATLAS:2024lda}.
In order to achieve stronger exclusion bounds in the parameter space, we want to reduce the theoretical uncertainty on the cross section.
It is therefore necessary to consider specific processes, and calculate their cross sections to higher orders in perturbation theory analytically.
This is the main topic of this thesis, where we consider the production of scalar leptons---the sleptons---in proton--proton collisions.

Protons are composed primarily of quarks and gluons, which carry color charge. Hence the dominant interaction in proton--proton collisions come from the strong interaction.
Therefore, when considering slepton pair production at hadron colliders, the primary focus so far has been on higher-order corrections in \gls{qcd}, which have been calculated at \gls{nlo}~\cite{Baer:1997nh} with resummation of large logarithms to \gls{nll} accuracy at \gls{lp}~\cite{Bozzi:2006fw,Bozzi:2007qr,Bozzi:2007tea,Fiaschi:2018xdm} and \gls{ll} accuracy at \gls{nlp}~\cite{Braseth:2025ylj}.

Of course, as we increase the \gls{qcd} precision to higher orders in perturbation and resummation, the added contribution from new terms will be smaller than the previous.
Therefore, the following question naturally arises:
\begin{quote}
    \textit{At what point is the added contribution from higher precision in \gls{qcd} so small that electroweak corrections at \gls{nlo} become important?}
\end{quote}
The main purpose of the research presented in this thesis is to provide an answer to this question.
Higher-order \gls{qft} calculations are convoluted and time-consuming, and answering this question helps guide our efforts toward the contributions that matter most.

We will consider a subset of the full electroweak corrections to slepton pair production in proton--proton collisions by calculating the inclusive cross section at \gls{nlo} in \gls{qed}. This process is---to the best of our knowledge---not considered in existing literature.

Although the collision of protons is too complicated to be handled analytically in its entirety, it can be dealt with by means of factorization~\cite{Ellis:1996mzs}, writing the full hadronic cross section as a convolution between experimentally obtained \glspl{pdf} and perturbatively calculable partonic cross sections.
The partonic cross sections are then regular cross sections for high-energy hard scattering processes between proton constituents---mostly quarks and gluons---and can be calculated analytically in \gls{qft}.

The majority of the work presented in this thesis concerns itself with the calculation of the partonic cross section.
As is characteristic for higher-order \gls{qft} calculations, we will encounter various \gls{uv} and \gls{ir} divergences.
These are dealt with using dimensional regularization~\cite{tHooft:1972tcz,Bollini:1972ui}, and eliminated using appropriate methods.
After obtaining a finite result for the partonic cross section we integrate numerically over the \glspl{pdf} to obtain the full hadronic cross section.

When doing calculations we will encounter many Feynman diagrams. The diagrams presented in this thesis have been drawn using \texttt{TikZ-Feynman}~\cite{Ellis:2016jkw}.


\section*{Thesis Outline}
The thesis is structured as follows:
\begin{description}
    \item[Chapter 1:] We start with a brief review of supersymmetry, its motivation and properties, with focus on the \gls{mssm} and sleptons. We also derive the necessary Feynman rules for sleptons from the \gls{mssm} Lagrangian.
    
    \item[Chapter 2:] The Feynman diagrams considered in this thesis give rise to divergent behavior.
    In this chapter we provide a discussion of the different kinds of divergences that appear in our calculations, and how they can be removed to allow theoretical predictions to remain finite.
    We also show how we can avoid calculating the most complicated loop integrals by recognizing them as Passarino--Veltman functions that can be evaluated efficiently by existing code libraries.

    \item[Chapter 3:] This chapter initiates the novel work presented in this thesis.
    Here we apply the theory from the preceding chapters to analytically calculate the \gls{nlo} \gls{qed} corrections to the inclusive partonic cross section for slepton pair production in the hard scattering of quark--anti-quark pairs.

    \item[Chapter 4:] In this chapter we bridge the gap between the perturbative hard scattering calculated in the previous chapter and the full hadronic cross section.
    We introduce the concept of \glspl{pdf}, including their renormalization, and write down the hadronic cross section for slepton pair production in terms of integrals over \glspl{pdf} that can be evaluated numerically.

    \item[Chapter 5:] At last, we compute the hadronic cross section by numerically integrating over the \glspl{pdf}. Results are presented and discussed for the production of first-generation sleptons---the selectrons---and are compared to state-of-the-art corrections from \gls{qcd}, before we conclude.
\end{description}
